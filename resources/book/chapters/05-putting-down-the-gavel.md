There's a woman I'll call Marla who used to sit two seats down from me in an early Tuesday meeting, and for about three months I couldn't stand her. Not because of anything she did to me directly. It was the way she talked about her ex-husband — every single share, no matter what the topic was supposed to be, found its way back to what he'd done, how selfish he was, how anyone with eyes could see he was the reason her life had fallen apart. I remember thinking, with total confidence, she is never going to get better until she stops blaming him and looks at herself. I had her whole recovery diagnosed inside of six weeks of listening to her talk.

Here's what I didn't know, because I never asked: her ex-husband had, in fact, done something close to unforgivable, something I only learned about a year later from someone else in the room. My verdict on Marla — she's stuck in blame, she won't do the work — wasn't insight. It was a guess dressed up as certainty, delivered by someone who had access to maybe four percent of the actual picture and felt no need to go find the other ninety-six.

I bring this up first because I want you to notice something before we go any further: passing that judgment didn't cost Marla anything. She never knew about it. It cost me. It cost me the chance to actually understand what was happening in that room, and — this is the part I didn't see at the time — it cost me a little bit of my own peace, because holding a verdict on someone takes energy, the same way holding a grudge does, which we'll get to properly in the next chapter. This chapter is about a much older habit than gossip or gut reactions. It's about the reflex to reach for a gavel, and why that reflex is so much more familiar to you than you probably think.

## Two Words Wearing the Same Coat

English has a problem, and it's worth naming before we go any further, because the whole chapter turns on it. The word "judgment" is doing two completely different jobs, and we use the same word for both without noticing the switch.

There's judgment as discernment — the capacity to look clearly at a situation and form an accurate assessment of it. Is this food spoiled? Is this person trustworthy with my car keys? Is this a good time to have a hard conversation with my sponsor? That kind of judgment is not optional. You need it constantly, and a life without it isn't peaceful, it's reckless. Nobody in this book — not Epictetus, not the Gospels, not the Big Book — is asking you to stop discerning.

Then there's judgment as verdict — a ruling on a person's worth, delivered as though you were the court and they were the defendant and the case is closed. She's selfish. He's a lost cause. I'm a fraud who doesn't deserve this program. That kind of judgment isn't assessing a situation. It's sentencing a person, usually on evidence you wouldn't accept in any other context, and usually forever.

The switch from the first kind to the second happens so fast most people never catch it happening. You start out discerning — that behavior hurt me — and by the end of the sentence you've sentenced — and that means she's a bad person. The first half might be completely accurate. The second half is a different claim entirely, one you almost never have enough information to make, about someone whose whole interior life you can't actually see.

This chapter is about that second kind, the verdict kind, and a claim that's going to sound strange until we walk through it: passing that kind of judgment — on someone else, or on yourself — is not really about the person being judged. It's a control strategy. It's one more version of the same move Chapter 4 spent a whole chapter naming: reaching into Pile Two, the pile of things that were never yours to manage, and trying to force it into shape anyway.

## Judgment as a Grab for Control

Here's the mechanism, and once you see it you won't be able to unsee it. You cannot control what another person did. You cannot control who they are underneath it, what they were carrying that day, what happened to them that made them capable of doing what they did. All of that is Pile Two, sealed shut, permanently outside your reach. But you can control the verdict. You can decide, right now, in your own head, exactly what kind of person they are — and the moment you do, something in you relaxes, because a mystery has been closed into a category. She's selfish. He's toxic. They'll never change. It feels like clarity. It's actually closure purchased on the cheap, and what you're really doing is trading the discomfort of not being able to control someone for the comfort of at least being able to control your story about them.

That's the whole trick. A verdict can't touch the person it's about. It only touches you — it just doesn't feel that way, because it's dressed up as an observation about them instead of a decision you made about how to feel.

Self-judgment runs the identical circuit, aimed inward, and it's the more common of the two by a wide margin in early recovery. You can't control the fact that you did what you did. The relapse happened. The thing you said to your kid happened. The years you can't get back are gone. All of that is sealed in the past, which Chapter 4 already told you is about as Pile Two as anything gets. But you can control the sentence — and so, with a kind of grim efficiency, a lot of people in early sobriety hand down the harshest possible verdict on themselves, fast, before anyone else gets the chance to. I'm irredeemable. I'm the kind of person who does this. I'll always be this way. It feels like accountability. It functions like control, snatched back from a situation where you genuinely had none, and it costs almost exactly what judging Marla cost me — except aimed at yourself, it costs a great deal more.

## What Epictetus and Marcus Actually Said About This

The Stoics wrote about this constantly, and it's some of their most quietly radical material, because it asks you to give up something that feels like moral clarity.

Marcus Aurelius, writing to himself, keeps returning to a version of the same instruction: when someone wrongs you, remind yourself that they did it because they believed, however wrongly, that it was somehow good or necessary — nobody sets out to do wrong on purpose, as they understand it. That's not an excuse for the behavior. It's a correction to your own understanding of what's actually happening in front of you. The person who cut you off in traffic isn't a monster who woke up plotting your inconvenience. They're a person operating from a mistaken judgment — maybe that they're late for something that matters, maybe that other drivers don't count as real people the way they do — and that mistaken judgment is a form of ignorance, not villainy. Recall Chapter 2: Stoicism's word for the root problem was ignorance, a wrong judgment about what's good. This is that same idea, now aimed outward instead of inward. The person who wronged you is running the same broken software you've run plenty of times yourself.

This does something specific and useful if you actually let it land: it doesn't excuse the act, but it dissolves the verdict on the person, because a verdict requires the belief that they saw clearly and chose evil anyway. Take that belief away — replace it with "they were operating from confusion, the same confusion I've operated from" — and the gavel has nothing left to come down on. You can still say the behavior was wrong. You can still set a boundary, still protect yourself, still hold someone accountable for what they did. What you lose is the extra step, the sentencing, the part where you decide who they are all the way down. And that extra step, Marcus would say, was never yours to take. Their character isn't in Pile One. Only your response is.

Epictetus makes almost the identical move about self-judgment, and this is the part that surprises people, because Stoicism has a reputation for being hard on the self. It isn't, not in the way people expect. Epictetus's advice, over and over, is to treat your own failures the way a good teacher treats a student's mistakes: correct the error, and move on, without the theatrical self-punishment. He's blunt about the fact that most people are far harsher judges of themselves than the situation calls for, and that this harshness doesn't produce better behavior — it produces paralysis, or a kind of performative misery that substitutes for actual change. The gavel coming down on yourself feels like moral seriousness. Epictetus would say it's usually just self-will run riot, wearing a mask of humility.

## You Are Not the Judge in the Room

The line everyone half-remembers from the Gospels is "judge not, that ye be not judged," and it gets used constantly — sometimes correctly, sometimes as a thought-terminating shield against ever having an opinion about anything. Neither of those uses is quite what the passage is doing.

Read the fuller version and the logic is more specific than "never evaluate anything." Jesus follows the line about judgment with the image of a man trying to pick a speck out of his brother's eye while ignoring the plank in his own — which isn't an argument against ever noticing specks. It's an argument about sequence and standing. You are, structurally, in a terrible position to sit in judgment on someone else's failures, because you cannot see your own with anything close to the clarity you imagine you have. The verdict you're so ready to hand down on them is being written by a judge who hasn't read his own case file.

There's a deeper claim underneath that, and it's the one this book keeps circling back to: the seat of final judgment isn't empty, waiting for you to fill it. It's occupied. "Judge not" isn't primarily a rule about being nice. It's a boundary about jurisdiction — a reminder that deciding a person's ultimate worth was never a job description that got handed to you, in the same way that Chapter 4 told you your reputation and other people's opinions were never yours to manage. You don't get the seat. Nobody does, except the one this tradition says already holds it, and holds it with an amount of mercy attached that should make you nervous about how eagerly you were about to occupy it yourself.

What this frees you to do, and it's not nothing, is discern without sentencing. You can still say clearly: that was a lie, that hurt me, that pattern is dangerous and I'm going to protect myself from it. That's the plank-and-speck passage working correctly — it never tells you to go blind, only to stop pretending you have the standing to write final verdicts. What it takes off the table is the part where you also decide, permanently and totally, who they are underneath the act. That part was never offered to you in the first place.

And this applies to the verdict you hand down on yourself with exactly the same force, maybe more. If the seat is occupied and it isn't you, that's true of your own case too. The relentless internal prosecutor a lot of people carry into early sobriety — the one that re-tries the same offenses nightly and never once returns a verdict of grace — is not, in this tradition's own account, doing God's job. It's doing a job nobody assigned, badly, to a defendant who's already exhausted.

## Principles Before Personalities, and the Sponsor Who Wouldn't Let Me Finish

The Steps have their own, more specific vocabulary for this, and it shows up in two places worth separating out, because they solve slightly different problems.

The first is a phrase you'll hear in almost any well-run meeting: principles before personalities. It's usually invoked as a cross-talk rule — don't diagnose the person who just shared, respond to the principle in what they said, not to your opinion of them — but it's really a working application of the same jurisdiction boundary the Gospels are describing. The room isn't a courtroom and nobody in it is qualified to be the judge, including you, especially about someone whose whole story you got in a four-minute share. I think about Marla now and wish someone had said that phrase to me directly, because it would have named exactly what I was doing.

The second place is closer to home, and it's the bigger one. I had a sponsor early on who cut me off mid-sentence more than once, and it was always at the same kind of moment — the moment I started narrating my own moral biography in the harshest available terms. I remember starting a share with something like, "I'm honestly just a selfish person, that's the real problem, I always have been," fully expecting this to read as accountability. He stopped me and said something I didn't understand yet: "That's not an inventory. That's a verdict. Go back and do the inventory."

That distinction is Step Four in miniature, and it's a precise, almost technical difference once you see it. A moral inventory is a list: here's what happened, here's what I did, here's the harm, here's the pattern I can see now that I couldn't see then. It's specific, dated, and it stops. A moral verdict is a single collapsed sentence that swallows the whole list and replaces it with an identity: I'm a selfish person. Notice what that sentence does that an inventory doesn't — it closes the case. There's nothing left to look at once you've decided what kind of person you fundamentally are. An inventory, by contrast, stays open, because a list of specific actions can always have new items added and — this is the part that matters — can always change going forward, in a way that a verdict about your essential nature cannot. Step Four asks for the list on purpose, not the sentence, because the list is the only one of the two that leaves you anywhere to go.

This is also, not incidentally, why shame and guilt behave so differently in recovery, even though people use the words as if they're interchangeable. Guilt says I did a bad thing. Shame says I am a bad thing. Guilt can be worked with — it's specific, it's actionable, you can make amends to it, it's Pile One material dressed as a feeling. Shame is a verdict, full stop, and there's no ninth step for a verdict. You can't make amends to a global sentence about your own worth; the sentence doesn't take input. That's precisely why shame tends to produce the opposite of the change it seems to be pushing toward — it tends to produce secrecy, because if you've already decided you're fundamentally the kind of person who does this, disclosure just confirms the sentence rather than opening anything up. Guilt sends you toward your sponsor. Shame sends you toward the door, or toward the thing you were trying to stop doing in the first place, because if the verdict's already been handed down, there's nothing left to protect by staying honest.

## A Second Scene, Closer to the Bone

I want to give you the self-judgment version in the same amount of detail I gave you Marla, because it's the one I think will land harder, and it's the one I've watched do more damage in early recovery than almost anything else in this book.

Eight months sober, a man I'd gotten close to at that same Tuesday meeting — I'll call him Danny — picked up. One drink, at a wedding, gone by the next morning, back in the rooms within a week telling anyone who'd listen. What happened in that week is the part worth slowing down on. He didn't call anyone the night it happened, even though calling was the single most obviously useful thing he could have done and he knew it. When I asked him later why not, he didn't describe a decision. He described a verdict that had already been rendered, instantly, before he'd even finished the drink: "I just thought, well, there it is, I knew I was a fraud the whole time, I knew this was going to happen eventually, so what's the point of calling anyone now."

Look at the shape of that sentence. It isn't guilt — guilt would have had him reaching for the phone within the hour, mortified and honest. It's a verdict, delivered instantly and with something close to relief, because a verdict resolves the unbearable uncertainty of not knowing what kind of person you are. Danny had, without knowing it, spent eight months quietly braced for exactly this outcome, and some part of him experienced the relapse less as a catastrophe and more as a confirmation — the gavel finally coming down on a case he'd been silently prosecuting the entire time he was sober. The drink didn't create the verdict. The verdict, held for eight months in reserve, is a lot of what made the week of silence afterward feel not just easy but almost natural.

We didn't spend our first conversation after that talking about the drink. We spent it doing what my old sponsor once made me do: separating the inventory from the sentence. What actually happened — one drink, at a wedding, under specific pressures we could name — from what he'd decided it meant about who he permanently was. The first list was short and workable. The second sentence had already cost him a week he didn't have to lose.

## Practice: Catch the Verdict, Retry the Verdict

This chapter's practice has two halves, because judging others and judging yourself are the same mechanism running in two directions, and it's worth catching each one separately until the pattern becomes familiar enough to catch in the moment, not just after the fact.

### Part One — Catch the Verdict (aimed outward)

1. Think of someone you've formed a firm opinion about recently — in a meeting, in your family, in traffic, anywhere. Write down the verdict in one plain sentence. ("He's toxic." "She'll never change.")

2. Underneath it, write down the actual evidence — only what you directly observed, nothing you inferred about their character or their reasons. Notice how much smaller this list is than the verdict felt.

3. Ask, honestly: what don't I know about what's underneath this? What might be true about their situation that I have no access to? You don't need an answer. You need to feel the size of what you don't know.

4. Rewrite the sentence as discernment instead of a verdict — a statement about the behavior and your own boundary, with the sentencing removed. Not "she's selfish," but "that behavior hurt me, and here's what I'm going to do about it." Notice that the second version is more useful than the first, not less honest.

### Part Two — Retry the Verdict (aimed inward)

1. Think of something about yourself you've already sentenced — a global statement about who you are, built out of something you did. ("I'm a fraud." "I'm selfish." "I always ruin this.")

2. Do the actual inventory underneath it, the way Step Four asks: what specifically happened, when, and what was the harm — to yourself or someone else. Keep it to facts you could say out loud to your sponsor without flinching.

3. Look at the gap between the list and the sentence. The list is usually workable — a handful of specific, dated actions. The sentence is usually a life sentence disguised as self-awareness. Notice which one actually gives you somewhere to go tomorrow.

4. Say the inventory version out loud to someone, today if you can. Not the verdict — the list. Verdicts survive in silence. Inventories are built to be spoken.

Do this enough times, in both directions, and something starts to loosen that most people don't expect: the world gets less exhausting to move through, not because you've stopped noticing what people do, or what you've done, but because you've stopped doing the extra, uninvited work of deciding what it all means about who everyone permanently is. That work was never yours. Putting the gavel down isn't the same as refusing to see clearly. It's refusing a job you were never actually qualified for, and never actually assigned — and it turns out to be one of the heaviest things this book is going to ask you to set down.
