## About the Author

I got sober on December 10, 2018. I was fifty years old, and it took me that long to finally get myself help.

Before that: over thirty years of drinking. The last few of them, morning until night, every single day. I was what people call a "functioning alcoholic," which is a polite way of saying I was good enough at hiding it, and smart enough — or thought I was smart enough — to keep telling myself it wasn't a problem yet. It was a problem the whole time.

I spent the next eight years in AA, plus time around NA — visiting, speaking to people going through it, showing up for Renewal Day about once a month when I could make it. Those are the rooms where I've heard the stories, over and over. Somewhere in all of that, I started noticing something I couldn't unsee. A lot of people — good people, people trying hard — hit a plateau and stayed there. Sober, technically. Alive, technically. But it was "one day at a time" as agony, not as a philosophy — white-knuckling their way to the next meeting, keeping track of who they had to call before the craving won. Working the Steps because a sponsor told them to, in the order a sponsor told them to, without ever quite getting why any of it was supposed to bring them peace. They weren't drinking anymore, but the resentments, the fear, the old bonds to whatever hurt them in the first place — none of that had actually let go. It was just being managed instead of resolved. Managed forever, one day at a time, forever.

Somewhere in that same stretch, I got curious about Stoicism — mostly because I realized I didn't actually know what the word meant. I thought it meant something like calm and collected, unbothered, stiff upper lip. So I started reading to find out, and what I found instead was a two-thousand-year-old philosophy built almost entirely around the exact problem I'd been circling in meetings and in church: what's actually yours to control, and what never was. That's when I noticed I already had this same conversation running on two other fronts. My father is a devout born-again Christian, and I sat next to him in church long before I understood a word of what was being said up front. My aunt studies the Bible and the Torah seriously — original Hebrew, original Greek — and we've spent years going back and forth about what those texts actually say versus what gets repeated about them. Between my father's church, my aunt's original-language study, the rooms, and the Stoics I'd stumbled into almost by accident, I kept running into the same idea wearing three different outfits. That's the real origin of this book. Not a decision to write something — the slow accumulation of finding the same answer in three places that had no business agreeing with each other.

That's what this book is trying to fix. Not the drinking — the part underneath the drinking. I want people to stop needing the fail-safes: the emergency call, the meeting you drag yourself to because the alternative is worse. Those things save lives, mine included, and I'm not telling anyone to walk away from what keeps them alive. I'm saying there's a further place to get to, where you're not white-knuckling the dichotomy of control — where you actually know, in your gut and not just in a slogan, what's yours to work on and what never was. That's the only real serenity I've found. Everything else was a rehearsal for it.

I wrote this from an alcoholic's seat, because that's the seat I sat in. But I don't think this is only about alcohol. Whatever you use to numb it, outrun it, or bury it — I hope this translates. The mechanism is the same regardless of what's in the bottle, the bag, or the behavior.

One more thing, because I'd rather you hear it from me than wonder: some of the stories in this book are true events, with names and identifying details changed to protect the people in them. Others are parables — composites built from patterns I've seen over and over, not any one person's story — written that way on purpose, so nobody's specific pain gets put on display just to make a point.

And here's the last thing, and it's the thing I most want you to believe: I am not the judge of you. I've sat in the rooms and heard the same story come around again — someone back in treatment, someone starting over for the third or fifth or tenth time — and I know that feeling from both sides, telling it and hearing it. That story doesn't disqualify you from anything. I'm not here to grade how you're doing it. I'm here because I want to help you tell a different story — one that doesn't loop, one that actually goes somewhere. A happy one, if we're lucky. That's the whole reason this book exists.

## Introduction: Before You Get Defensive

You already know some of where I've been, if you read the page before this one — the drinking, the rooms, my father's church, my aunt's Hebrew and Greek. What I haven't told you yet is where I actually stand on the three of them, together, and I think that's worth saying plainly before you read another page — because I think most books like this one dodge it.

I owe the 12 Steps a debt I'm not interested in downplaying. I've sat in a lot of different meetings since — big rooms and small ones, church basements and rec centers, each with its own personality depending on who was running it and who showed up that day — and the program, the people in it, and the structure it gave me almost certainly kept me alive in the early years. It's still part of how I stay sober today. The church is the same kind of debt, just older — I was in a pew next to my father before I understood a word of what was being preached, and years of conversation with an aunt who reads the Bible and the Torah in their original languages have shaped how I think about grace more than any single sermon has. And the Stoics came last, almost by accident, when I went looking for a plain definition of a word I thought I already understood — calm, collected, unbothered — and found something closer to a full diagnosis of the same problem the other two had already handed me, just older, and in different words.

Along the way, sitting in that many different rooms, I noticed something worth naming honestly, even though it's not a comfortable thing to say out loud in a fellowship built on gratitude for meetings. I've met people decades into sobriety — one man in particular, more than forty years sober, well into retirement, with nowhere he had to be — who was still at a meeting nearly every single day. Some of that was fellowship, and good company is nothing to apologize for. But watching him, I got the strong sense it was also something closer to fear: not "I want to be here," but "I can't not be here" — the same white-knuckle grip on sobriety he'd had in year one, just wearing a more socially acceptable outfit forty years later. That's not a criticism of him, or of meetings, or of anyone who needs that structure to stay alive — it kept me alive too. It's an observation about where I hope this book can take people past: not away from the rooms, but to the point where walking into one is a choice made in freedom, not a rope you're still gripping because you're afraid of what happens if you let go. I believe I've reached that point myself, and I wrote this book, in part, because I want other people to find their way there too.

So let me be direct: I agree with the overwhelming majority — somewhere in the range of eighty to ninety-nine percent, depending on the day and the specific claim — of what each of these three traditions teaches. This book is not a takedown of the Steps, a watered-down substitute for faith, or Stoicism repackaged as self-help. I respect all three enough to have actually lived inside them, not just read about them. If you're deeply committed to one of these traditions, I'm not writing this to talk you out of it. In several real ways, I'm one of you.

But I also want to be honest that I don't agree with every part of any of the three, and I'd guess — if you're honest too — neither do you. Even at ninety-nine percent, that last one percent is real. Even at eighty, that last twenty is real. The Steps, taken alone, can slide into a kind of learned helplessness if you let the word "powerless" travel further than it's meant to. Certain strands of religious teaching can slide into shame that never resolves into grace. Certain readings of Stoicism can slide into a coldness that mistakes numbness for peace. Disagreeing with a piece of a tradition isn't the same as rejecting it, and I'm not interested in pretending otherwise just to keep everyone comfortable.

## A Confession About This Paragraph

Here's what I've noticed, and it's the reason this introduction exists at all: some part of you may have bristled somewhere in the last four paragraphs. Maybe at "I don't agree with every part." Maybe at putting a fellowship, a faith, and a philosophy in the same sentence like they're comparable, when for you one of them isn't just a tool among tools — it's the truth, full stop, and the others are, at best, partial. If something in you tightened up reading that, I'm not going to tell you that reaction is wrong. I'm going to tell you it's the whole subject of this book, already at work on you.

I'm not asking you to abandon the conviction that your tradition holds something the others don't. I hold that conviction too, about more than one of these three. I'm asking you to notice the tightening, name it for what it is, and keep reading anyway — the same practice this book is going to ask of you everywhere else, applied here first, on the page, before it's asked of you anywhere harder.

## What This Book Is Actually Claiming

Not that Christianity, Stoicism, and the 12 Steps are the same thing wearing different clothes. They aren't, and I'm not going to pretend a fellowship's practical program, a philosophical system, and a faith built around the person of Christ are theologically or philosophically interchangeable — they make different claims about what's ultimately true, and those differences are real and worth taking seriously on their own terms.

What I am claiming is narrower and, I think, sturdier: that on the specific question of why we suffer and what to do about it day to day, these three traditions converge on strikingly similar practical instructions, arrived at independently. That convergence is worth your attention regardless of which of the three, if any, you consider ultimately true. You can use every tool in this book as a committed 12-Step member, a committed Christian, a committed Stoic, all three at once, or none of the above — because the tools were never the property of one tradition to begin with. Each just wrote its own instructions for the same discipline.

Take what works. Argue with the rest. That's not a loophole in this book — it's the first practice in it.
